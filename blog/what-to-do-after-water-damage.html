What to Do After Water Damage in Your Hollywood Home

Knowing what to do after water damage is the difference between a bad afternoon and a five-figure renovation. Here is the part most guides skip: the worst destruction rarely happens while water is pouring in. It happens in the quiet hours afterward — while wet drywall sits, water wicks two feet up a wall cavity, and South Florida humidity turns a damp room into a mold farm. The sequence below is the order that actually limits damage, whether you are dealing with a burst supply line in Emerald Hills or a roof leak in Hollywood Lakes.

What to Do After Water Damage in the First 15 Minutes

Forget the mop for now. The first quarter-hour is about safety and stopping the bleeding, in that order:

  1. Do not walk into standing water blind. If water has reached outlets, power strips, or appliances, treat the room as energized until proven otherwise. Rubber flip-flops are not protective equipment.
  2. Kill the power if you can do it safely. If the breaker panel is dry and reachable without stepping through water, shut off the affected circuits. If the panel itself is wet or you would have to wade to reach it, do not touch it — call an electrician and stay out.
  3. Stop the source. Supply line or fixture leak? Turn the fixture shutoff valve, usually under the sink or behind the toilet. Cannot find it, or the pipe itself burst? Go straight to the main water shutoff — typically near the meter by the street or where the line enters the house — and turn it clockwise until it stops.
  4. Move what matters. Electronics, important papers, rugs, and anything irreplaceable go to high, dry ground. Do not spend these minutes on things that can be replaced.
  5. Call for help while the water is still visible. A restoration crew wants to see the loss as it is, not your best cleanup effort. If standing water is still spreading, emergency water extraction comes before anything cosmetic.

Document Everything Before Anything Dries

Your future insurance adjuster was not there. Your photos are the entire case. Before you move anything major, walk the affected areas with your phone and capture wide shots of every room, close-ups of the source if visible, water lines on walls and furniture, and serial plates on damaged appliances. Shoot a slow video narrating what you see — "this is the hallway at 3:40, water coming from under the bathroom door" — because you will forget details by tomorrow.

Write down the time you discovered the water, when you shut it off, and who you called. Email the photos to yourself so they are timestamped off your phone. One detail that matters more than people realize: knowing the three categories of water contamination helps you describe the loss accurately. "Clean supply-line water in the hallway" versus "sewage backup in the bathroom" changes everything about how the claim — and the cleanup — gets handled.

The Five Mistakes That Turn a Small Leak Into a Big Claim

After years of watching these jobs, the expensive ones almost always involve one of these:

  • The shop-vac heroics. A wet-dry vac handles a spilled bucket. It does not handle forty gallons across a hallway, and it does nothing for water inside the wall. Worse, it gives you false confidence that the problem is solved.
  • "It looks dry." Drywall can look fine while the insulation behind it is soaked. Press your palm against the lower wall — cool to the touch means wet. In our humidity, hidden moisture becomes a mold problem within days, not weeks.
  • Cranking the AC to "dry it out." Air conditioning dehumidifies, but it also moves air — potentially spreading contaminants from a gray- or black-water loss through your ductwork. Do not run the system until the water category is known.
  • Tearing things out yourself. Ripping up carpet or cutting drywall before documentation can damage an insurance claim and, with contaminated water, expose you to things you do not want to breathe.
  • Waiting until Monday. Water does not observe business hours, and neither does microbial growth. A Friday-night leak left alone becomes a Monday-morning demolition.

When the Water Stops: Drying Is a Separate Job

Getting the visible water out is roughly half the work. The other half — the part that decides whether you get mold, buckling floors, or a musty smell that never leaves — is drying the structure itself: the wall cavities, the subfloor, the sill plates. That takes moisture meters, not guesswork, and dehumidifiers sized to the space, not a box fan from the garage. If a company tells you the job is done because the floor looks dry, get a second opinion.

What to Do After Water Damage in a Condo or Rental

Different building, different rules. If you rent, notify your landlord or property manager in writing immediately — but do not wait for permission to stop active water. Preventing further damage is both your obligation and your right. In a condo, alert the association too, because water rarely respects unit boundaries: your leak is your downstairs neighbor's ceiling stain. Document common-area damage (hallways, lobbies) separately, since HOA master policies and your own HO-6 policy split responsibility at the walls.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long do I have before mold becomes a problem?

In South Florida's heat and humidity, mold can begin colonizing damp drywall and other organic materials within 24 to 48 hours. That does not mean every damp room grows mold — but it does mean drying should not wait for a convenient day.

Should I call my insurance company first or a restoration company?

Stop the water first, document second, then call both. Most policies require you to mitigate further damage, so waiting on an adjuster before acting can actually hurt your claim. A restoration company's documentation usually becomes the backbone of the claim file.

Can I handle water damage cleanup myself?

A small, clean-water spill — a tipped-over bucket, a minor sink overflow you caught immediately — is fine to handle yourself with towels and airflow. Anything involving saturated drywall, carpet pad, insulation, or unknown contamination belongs to professionals with extraction equipment and moisture meters.

The water came from my neighbor's unit. Who pays?

Start with documentation and notify both your neighbor and the HOA or landlord in writing. Responsibility usually follows the source, but your own policy may cover your damages first and sort out liability later. Do not let finger-pointing delay the drying.

Will my homeowner's insurance cover water damage?

Sudden and accidental losses — burst pipes, appliance failures, storm-driven roof leaks — are typically covered. Gradual leaks ignored for months, and rising floodwater (which needs separate flood insurance), usually are not. When in doubt, file and let the adjuster decide; just keep mitigating in the meantime.
