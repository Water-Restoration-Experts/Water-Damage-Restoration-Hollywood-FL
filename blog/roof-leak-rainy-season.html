Roof Leaks During Hollywood’s Rainy Season

In Hollywood, the rainy season does not knock politely. From roughly May through October, the afternoons stack up towering storms that dump inches of rain in an hour, and every one of those storms is a stress test for your roof. A roof leak during rainy season rarely announces itself with drama — it starts as a faint stain on the ceiling, a musty corner in a closet, or paint bubbling over a window. By the time water is dripping into a bucket, the leak has usually been working for weeks.

Here is what South Florida homeowners should know about how these leaks start, how the water travels once it is inside, and what to do while the rain is still falling.

Why Rainy Season Finds Every Weak Roof

It is not one storm that causes most roof leaks — it is the accumulation. Day after day of intense afternoon rain keeps roofing materials saturated. Underlayment that would dry out between storms in a drier climate never gets the chance. Seams, flashing, and penetrations around vents and skylights sit wet for months, and the constant expansion and contraction from heat and downpours works sealant loose.

South Florida adds its own accelerants. Wind-driven rain does not fall straight down; it pushes sideways under shingles and tiles, into places gravity-fed rain would never reach. Tropical systems bring sustained wind that lifts edges just enough for water to get underneath. And the UV beating down between storms bakes sealants and flashing brittle, so they fail exactly when the next storm arrives.

The Stain Lies: How a Roof Leak Actually Travels

Here is the thing that confuses almost everyone: the wet spot on your ceiling is almost never directly below the roof breach. Water enters through a compromised shingle, cracked tile, or failed flashing, then travels — along the roof decking, down a rafter, across the top of drywall — sometimes ten or fifteen feet from where it got in. It pools at the lowest point or the first seam it finds, and that is where your ceiling stains.

This is why "just patch above the stain" fails so often. The real entry point can be upslope, around a vent boot, or at a flashing joint nowhere near the visible damage. Finding it requires getting eyes on the roof itself and tracing the path, ideally while it is still wet enough to follow. A roof leak cleanup assessment starts with that tracing, because drying the ceiling without addressing the entry is just scheduling the next leak.

What to Do While It Is Still Raining

You cannot safely fix a roof in a downpour, but you can limit the damage:

  1. Contain the interior drip. Buckets, towels, and moving furniture and electronics out of the path. Puncture a small hole in a bulging ceiling blister to let trapped water drain in a controlled way rather than collapsing the drywall — place the bucket first.
  2. Protect what is below. Water follows gravity into closets, onto wood floors, and into wall cavities. Move valuables and lay plastic sheeting over furniture you cannot move.
  3. Do not go on the roof. Wet tile and shingle roofs in Florida are treacherously slick, and lightning is a real risk during these storms. Interior containment is the job for today.
  4. Document the active leak. Video of water coming in, timestamps, and photos of the storm outside. If this becomes an insurance matter, "it was raining" is not documentation — footage is.

After the Storm Passes: What to Check

Once things dry out enough for safe access, the inspection matters more than the repair. Check the attic first: look for damp insulation, water stains on the underside of decking, daylight visible through gaps, and any musty smell. Insulation that has been wet often needs to go — it loses its insulating value and holds moisture against wood framing.

On the roof itself (or via a roofer), the usual suspects are: lifted or missing shingles and tiles, cracked flashing around chimneys and vents, deteriorated sealant at penetrations, and clogged valleys where debris dams water sideways. Gutters and downspouts deserve a look too — when they overflow, water sheets down exterior walls and finds its way in through windows and soffits.

The Interior Damage People Miss

A roof leak is a ceiling problem the way an iceberg is a tip problem. By the time the ceiling shows a stain, water has usually saturated the insulation above it, wicked into the top plates of the wall framing, and dampened the drywall from the back side — the side you cannot see. Ceiling leak cleanup that only repaints the stain is cosmetic; the wet assembly above it keeps feeding moisture into the structure.

This is also where mold gets its opening. Attic and ceiling-cavity spaces are warm, dark, and now wet — the exact conditions from our guide on how mold takes hold after water intrusion. If the leak ran for more than a couple of days, assume the hidden assembly needs drying or removal, not just a coat of stain-blocking primer.

When a Leak Becomes a Flood

Most rainy-season roof leaks are drips and stains. But a tropical system can turn a compromised roof into an interior flood — inches of standing water, saturated everything. At that point the job stops being a roof repair with some drying and becomes a full water-loss response: extraction, moisture mapping, structural drying, and documentation. Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my roof only leak during heavy rain, not light rain?

Light rain runs off before it can exploit small vulnerabilities. Heavy, wind-driven rain overwhelms marginal flashing, pushes water sideways under materials, and ponds in low spots — it finds the weaknesses that light rain never tests.

Can I just seal the leak from inside the attic?

Interior sealant is at best a temporary stopgap. Water will find another path around it, and you have sealed moisture into the assembly. The durable fix is always on the roof surface, at the actual entry point.

How long can a roof leak go unnoticed?

Months, easily. Slow leaks into attic insulation or wall cavities show nothing inside until the material saturates. That is why post-storm attic checks matter more than waiting for a ceiling stain.

Will my insurance cover a roof leak during rainy season?

It depends on the cause. Storm-created damage (wind-lifted shingles, impact) is generally covered; age-related wear is generally not. Documentation of the storm and the roof damage is what separates the two outcomes.

Should I call a roofer or a water damage company first?

If water is actively entering, address the interior damage first — containment and drying cannot wait for a roofing appointment. Then get the roofer to fix the entry point. The two jobs are sequential, not either-or.
